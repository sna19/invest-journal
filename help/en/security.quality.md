# Quality: how good the business is

**What it is.** How much the company earns on each unit of revenue and capital, and how much it depends on debt.

**The idea.** A good business earns a lot on the capital put in, year after year, and does not live on debt. Such a business survives bad years; a weak one does not, however cheap it is.

**How to use it.**

- **Gross margin** — the share of revenue left after the direct cost of the goods. Shows the product's strength: 70–90% in software, 20–40% in manufacturing, 20–30% in retail. Falling year after year — competition presses the prices.
- **Operating margin** — after all the costs of running the company (salaries, research, marketing). 15–20% and more — a strong business; under 5% — a thin cushion, any bad year brings a loss.
- **Net margin** — what is left for shareholders after interest and taxes. Compare with the operating margin: a big gap — expensive debt or taxes.
- **ROE** — earnings on shareholders' equity. About 15% and more — good, over 20% steadily for years — an excellent business. Check the debt: a high ROE with heavy debt is made with borrowed money.
- **ROA** — earnings on all assets. 1% is good for banks; 5–10% for industrial companies. Smooths out the effect of debt that inflates ROE.
- **Debt / equity** — debt per unit of equity. Under 50% — calm, 50–150% — usual for industry and utilities, over 200% — the company is exposed to rising rates and bad years. Do not apply to banks: debt is their raw material.
- **Current ratio** — current assets over the debts due within a year. Over 1.5 — there is a cushion; under 1 — money for current payments may run short (except retail, where it is normal).

**When it matters.** First thing before a purchase and at every quarterly report: margins and ROE falling several quarters in a row — a reason to open the thesis.

**Reminder.** First — is it a good business; only then — what it costs.

**Trap.** A high ROE from debt or buybacks: equity shrinks, ROE rises, and the business is no better.
